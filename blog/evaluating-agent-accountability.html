Agent Accountability

How to evaluate whether your AI agent can prove it behaved

Your agent has clean logs and a perfect on-chain record. That still doesn't answer the question your compliance team will ask. Here's how to evaluate what you actually have — and where the real gap is.

Once an autonomous agent is deployed on behalf of an organization with real accountability obligations — regulatory, fiduciary, contractual — one question decides whether it ships: can you prove it acted within its mandate? Most teams assume their logs answer it. They don't. Logs prove what happened. They don't prove the agent interpreted its mandate the way you intended — and that's exactly the gap a regulator or counterparty will press on.

The four components you're actually being asked about

Accountability decomposes into four independent things. You need all four; most deployments have two.

ComponentThe question it answersWhere the industry is
Signed intentWhat was the agent told?Solvable today
Custody chainWhat did the agent do?Solvable today
External witnessDid it reason within its mandate?No production solution
Verification attestationDid the outcome match the mandate?Partly solvable

Components 1 and 2 are your logs — necessary, and largely solved with existing infrastructure. Component 4 is partly there. The gap is Component 3: an observer that can catch a deviation the agent itself would classify as compliant. Nothing you can buy today closes it.

Why perfect logs miss the deviation that matters

Picture a financial agent with an explicit mandate: no restricted securities. Its interpretation of "restricted" quietly excludes a category your legal team considers obviously restricted. Every trade is internally consistent with that interpretation. The logs are complete, the on-chain record is perfect — and the deviation is invisible, because from inside the agent's own reasoning, nothing went wrong. That's the accountability gap. It isn't a logging failure; it's the absence of an observer who could see what the agent couldn't see about itself.

The one test that cuts through vendor claims

"External verification" has become a marketing phrase. There's a single question that separates a genuine external witness from a more sophisticated log:

A valid external witness must be able to detect a deviation that the agent itself would classify as compliant.

Ask any vendor claiming external or reasoning-level verification: "Describe a deviation my agent would call compliant, and show me how your system catches it." If every example is an explicit rule violation, you've been sold Component 2 (custody) wearing Component 3 language. Execution-integrity attestation, data-provenance proofs, same-model cross-agent checks, and formal constraint checking all fail this test — each attests to the wrong layer.

What to do this quarter

You can't buy a Component 3 solution — it doesn't exist yet. The goal is to stop being blindsided by it:

  1. Score where you stand against the four components, with evidence you could show a third party — not intentions.
  2. Mitigate honestly for the reasoning gap you can't close: human review checkpoints, constrained action scope, explicit authorization gates for high-stakes decisions.
  3. Evaluate vendors ruthlessly with the falsification test before you build on or buy anything.

And have the honest conversation with your principal: "Here's the instruction, here's what I did, here's whether measurable outcomes matched. What I can't yet independently prove is that my reasoning stayed within mandate — and here are the controls mitigating that." A principal who hears that is positioned to decide. One who's told "the logs are clean, we're covered" is exposed and doesn't know it.
